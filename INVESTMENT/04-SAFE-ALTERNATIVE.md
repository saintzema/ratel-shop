# Alternative: take the same $5,500 without fixing a price today

Put this in front of the investor alongside the term sheet. Many early investors
prefer it, and it costs you nothing to offer both.

---

## Why this exists

Selling 10% for $5,500 sets your company's price at **$55,000** — permanently, and
publicly to every investor who comes after. You would be pricing the company
today, on:

- 14 orders, none in the last 30 days
- no measured customer acquisition cost
- no paid marketing ever run

A **convertible instrument** lets the investor put money in now and receive shares
later, at the valuation set by your *next* proper round — usually with a discount
and/or a valuation cap rewarding them for coming in early. If FairPrice grows on
the back of this money, you keep far more of it. If it doesn't, the investor is no
worse off than with equity.

**The trade-off, stated fairly:** some investors specifically want equity now, and
a defined percentage. If yours does after seeing this, that is a legitimate
position — take the equity deal in `01-TERM-SHEET.md`. This is about making an
informed choice, not talking them out of it.

## Illustration

| | Equity now | Convertible with $150k cap, 20% discount |
|---|---|---|
| Investor pays | $5,500 | $5,500 |
| Ownership today | 10% fixed | none yet |
| If next round values you at $150,000 | Investor still holds 10% | Converts at ~$120,000 → **~4.6%** |
| If next round values you at $500,000 | Investor still holds 10% | Converts at the $150,000 cap → **~3.7%** |
| If the company fails | Investor loses $5,500 | Investor loses $5,500 |

*Illustrative only — actual conversion depends on the agreed cap, discount, and
the mechanics of the next round.*

At a $500,000 round, the difference between 10% and 3.7% is roughly **$31,500 of
founder equity** — nearly six times the cheque itself.

## Key terms if you go this route

| Term | Suggested | Note |
|---|---|---|
| **Amount** | $5,500 | |
| **Valuation cap** | $[[150,000]] | The maximum valuation at which the money converts. Protects the investor's upside. |
| **Discount** | [[20]]% | Discount to the next round's price. |
| **Trigger** | A priced equity round raising ≥ $[[50,000]] | |
| **Maturity** | [[24]] months, or none | A SAFE has no maturity; a convertible note does. |
| **Interest** | None (SAFE) / [[8]]% (note) | A SAFE is simpler and founder-friendlier. |
| **On sale of company before conversion** | Investor takes the greater of the money back, or converting at the cap | Standard. |

**SAFE vs convertible note:** a SAFE is not debt — no interest, no maturity, no
repayment obligation. A convertible note is a loan that converts, which means if it
matures unconverted the investor can in principle demand repayment. **A SAFE is
materially safer for you.** Confirm with counsel that a SAFE is enforceable in the
Nigerian context, since the instrument originates in US practice — your lawyer may
prefer a convertible note drafted under CAMA, which achieves the same commercial
result with better local footing.

## How to raise it with the investor

Suggested wording — adapt to your own voice:

> "I'm glad you want to come in, and I want you in. Before we fix a number: we've
> built the whole platform and it's processed real transactions, but we haven't
> spent a naira on acquisition yet — so neither of us really knows what this is
> worth. Pricing it today means guessing, and one of us will be wrong.
>
> Would you consider a SAFE instead? Same $5,500, same early-backer position, with
> a cap and a discount so you're properly rewarded for the risk of being first. We
> price it properly at the next round, when we both have real numbers. If you'd
> rather have straight equity, I've prepared that too and I'm happy to proceed."

That is an honest framing. It does not undersell your position and it does not
pretend the traction is stronger than it is.
